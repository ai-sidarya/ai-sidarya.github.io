From the Mahabharata · Bhishma's vow

The prince Devavrata could have been king. But his father wished to marry again, and the bride's family asked that her children inherit the throne. So Devavrata gave up his claim and vowed to serve the throne for life. People named him Bhishma, "the one of the terrible vow." He kept it through every storm that followed.

The chess lesson: a vow gives you roots. Choose your three openings with care, then keep your promise for twenty games. And note the epic's quiet warning: commit to what you have thought through, not to what you are afraid to question.

1 · Centre

3 min

A tree with many shallow roots falls in the first wind.

  1. Three slow breaths.
  2. Ask yourself: "What am I afraid to miss out on if I choose?" Name it. It is normal.
  3. Say: "I choose, and I commit."

2 · The Idea

10 min

Many players hop between openings, memorise moves they do not understand, and feel lost the moment the opponent steps off the path. That is how motivation dies.

The cure is a small, deliberate repertoire of three:

  1. One opening as White.
  2. One defence against 1.e4 (as Black).
  3. One defence against 1.d4 (as Black).

Learn each one as four things, not a list of moves:

StructureWhich pawns stand where, and what that means.
SetupWhere your pieces want to live and where your king goes.
PlanWhat you do when you are out of ideas. One sentence.
TrapThe one common tactic you must know, for either side.

The 20-game rule. Play each opening for 20 games before you judge it. A few losses mean you are learning, not that the opening is wrong.

This is character training too. Commitment is choosing despite the fear of missing out. It also takes courage to be a beginner again in a small corner of the game. That courage is how roots grow.

3 · Train

20 min

Step 1 · Meet your suggestions 5 min

These match temperament; they are starting points, not rankings. Any sound opening can be mastered. Choose by feel after trying them.

White: Scotch Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4) or Italian Game (3.Bc4).
vs 1.e4: 1…e5, the open games.
vs 1.d4: King's Indian Defence (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6).

Why it fits: open lines, quick development, real attacking chances.

White: London System (1.d4 with Bf4).
vs 1.e4: Caro-Kann Defence (1…c6).
vs 1.d4: Queen's Gambit Declined (1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6).

Why it fits: sturdy structures, clear setups, few early surprises.

White: Queen's Gambit (1.d4 d5 2.c4).
vs 1.e4: French Defence (1…e6).
vs 1.d4: Slav Defence (1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6).

Why it fits: pawn chains and long plans that reward understanding.

White: English Opening (1.c4) or Réti Opening (1.Nf3).
vs 1.e4: Sicilian Defence (1…c5).
vs 1.d4: Nimzo-Indian Defence (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4).

Why it fits: flexible, rich in counter-chances, many ways to outplay an opponent.

4 · Play

20 min

When the book ends, the player begins.

Play one or two games at a comfortable pace (10+5 or slower). Use your new openings whenever the chance arises. When your opponent leaves the path, do not panic. Go back to your four things (Structure, Setup, Plan, Trap) and to the Pause Routine from Lesson 2.

Your 20-game commitment

Press +1 after each game you play with an opening. Come back and tick them off over the coming weeks.

5 · Reflect

7 min

Boring depth beats shiny breadth.

Adapt this lesson to your age
Young wizard (6+)
Give each opening a story or a nickname. Keep only one idea per opening: "Where do my knights go? Where does my king go?" Let the child choose from two options so the choice feels like theirs.
Seasoned wizard (60+)
Favour openings with a clear logic over long forcing lines. Read master-game summaries aloud, and review your opening cards weekly. Repetition is your friend: slower, deeper recall is still strong recall.
