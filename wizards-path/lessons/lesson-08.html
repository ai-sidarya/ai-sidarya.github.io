Lesson 8 · 60 minutes

Hanuman's Tail

The smallest endings carry the greatest power.

From the Mahabharata · Bhima and Hanuman's tail

Bhima, the strongest of the Pandavas, was walking through the forest when he found an old, frail monkey lying across the path with his tail stretched out. Bhima ordered him to move it. The monkey replied he was too old and weak: Bhima could simply lift the tail aside himself.

Bhima tried. He used all his strength and could not shift it even a little. He bowed his head, and the monkey revealed himself as Hanuman, who blessed him.

The chess lesson: the quiet, small parts of the game, a lone king and a single pawn, can be immovable. Give the humble endgame your full respect.

1 · Centre

3 min

Strength that cannot bow cannot learn.

  1. Three slow breaths.
  2. Think of one small, quiet thing in your life that carries more weight than it seems.
  3. Say: "I give the small thing my full attention."

2 · The Idea

10 min

Endgames look small and quiet, like an old monkey lying across a path. They hold huge power. Many games that look equal are decided here, and the player who knows three simple ideas wins them.

1 · King
The king is a fighter. With few pieces on the board, he no longer hides. March him to the centre and toward the action.
2 · Square
The rule of the square. To check whether a king can stop a runaway pawn, draw a square from the pawn to its queening square. If the king steps into that square, he catches the pawn.
3 · Opposition
The kings face each other with one square between. The side that is not to move holds the opposition, and the other king must step aside.

Humility. Hanuman looked weak and was not. A lone pawn and a lone king look weak and are not. Treat every endgame, however small, with full attention. Respecting the small thing is a skill, and it wins games.

3 · Train

20 min

Drill A · The rule of the square 6 min

White's pawn on h4 wants to queen on h8, which is four moves away. The square runs d4–h4–h8–d8. White to move in both. Can the black king catch it?

Reveal the answer

Position 1: caught, a draw. Black's king on d5 is already inside the square. After h5 Ke5, h6 Kf6, h7 Kg7 the pawn falls.

Position 2: the pawn runs and queens. Black's king on c5 is one file outside the square. After h5 Kd6, h6 Ke7, h7 Kf7, h8=Q, the king is too late.

One step decides the game. That is the power of counting.

Drill B · The opposition 7 min

The same position twice, with a different side to move. White wants to win.

Reveal the answer

Position 3, White to move: a draw. White must step aside. Black holds the opposition.

Position 4, Black to move: White wins. Black must step aside, and White's king breaks through.

The kings are identical. Only whose turn it is changed. The player who does not have to move holds the power.

Drill C · A key square 4 min

White's king stands in front of his pawn, on e6. Who wins if White is to move? And if Black is to move?

Reveal the answer

White wins either way. Squares like e6, d6 and f6 are key squares for a pawn on e5: if your king reaches one, the pawn queens with your help. For example, after Kf6 Kf8 e6 Ke8 e7 Kd7, the king takes the f7 square and the pawn promotes.

Lesson: get your king in front of your pawn, not behind it.

Drill D · Audit your endgames 3 min

4 · Play

20 min

The small ending deserves the full warrior.

Three practice endgames. Open each position on an analysis board (the link is under each diagram) and play it out against a friend, an engine or yourself. Play the white side, then switch.

Practice 1. White wins. Goal: queen the pawn without losing it. Hint: keep your king in front.

Practice 2. It is Black's move. White wins with accurate play. Goal: hold the opposition and break through. As Black, try to hold the draw.

Practice 3. White wins by running the pawn at once. Goal: count it, then run.

If you also play a regular game today, use the Pause and the Report. When the game reaches an endgame, say aloud: "King to the centre."

Your style's endgame challenge

Use your king like a sword. You love the attack; in an endgame your king is the attacker. Bring him forward early.

Do not stay home. Your king is safe because few pieces remain. Walk him to the centre and trust yourself.

Plan the pawns. Count your pawn majority and decide where you can create a passed pawn. The plan is the whole game in an endgame.

Count, do not hope. Endgames are exact. Replace a swindle with arithmetic: the square, the opposition and the key squares.

5 · Reflect

7 min

The strongest players are those who can still respect the smallest piece.

Adapt this lesson to your age
Young wizard (6+)
Make the king a hero. Walk him across the board with a toy knight beside him. Use coins to mark the "square" so the child can see it. Let them race a pawn against a hand-held king.
Seasoned wizard (60+)
Endgames suit patient minds. Practise each position slowly, say the rule aloud, and replay it a few times over a week. Positions repeated by hand stay in memory.
