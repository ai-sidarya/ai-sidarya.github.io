Chapter 19: First 90 Days

Source statusDrafted with AI. Verify standards and product details against primary sources before relying on them.
TakeawayYour first 90 days decide whether you become a stabilizer or a source of noise. Assess first, act second.

1. What you must know Core

The first 90 days are about learning the system, not proving yourself. You assess before you act. Quick wins must be small, safe, and symbolic. Early big decisions often cause damage.

You must understand people, architecture, safety case, field issues, and commercial constraints. You must map decision rights, interfaces, and bottlenecks. You must learn how the team works under pressure.

A 30‑60‑90 plan structures your entry. You define what you learn, what you fix, and what you change. You ask each stakeholder sharp questions. You avoid early commitments that lock you into bad assumptions.

2. How it works Working

Assessment comes first. You observe meetings, read documents, and talk to people. You map architecture, safety case, test strategy, and field issues. You identify gaps and risks.

Quick wins build trust. They show you can help without disrupting. Examples include clarifying a decision, removing a small blocker, or improving a meeting ritual.

Decisions to avoid early include reorganizations, architecture changes, safety case rewrites, and major process shifts. You need evidence before you act.

Assess→Quick wins→Deep analysis→Structural decisions→Long-term plan

A 30‑60‑90 plan structures your entry. First 30 days: learn. Next 30: diagnose. Final 30: act. Stakeholder questions reveal expectations, risks, and hidden issues.

3. EPS example Working

Imagine joining an EPS program mid‑cycle. Steering feel complaints exist. Integration is slow. Teams are stretched. OEM is nervous.

First 30 days: you observe. You read architecture docs, safety case, calibration flows, and field data. You attend integration reviews. You ask engineers what slows them down. You ask OEM what worries them.

Next 30 days: you diagnose. You find unclear decision rights between software and calibration. You find missing combined maneuver tests. You find weak interface ownership for EPS–OEM CAN messages.

Final 30 days: you act. You clarify decision rights. You assign interface owners. You add missing tests. You improve integration rituals. You communicate clearly with OEM.

4. How it fails (concrete scenario) Interview depth

Consider a new manager who wants to “make an impact.” In week one, they reorganize teams, rewrite processes, and challenge architecture. They propose new steering feel modes without understanding calibration or safety case.

Teams panic. OEM sees instability. Integration slows. Safety case becomes inconsistent. People stop sharing concerns. Manager loses trust.

Another failure involves chasing quick wins that are actually big decisions. Manager changes test strategy without understanding coverage. They modify calibration review flow without knowing constraints. They break working parts of the system.

Stakeholder questions are shallow. Manager asks “How can I help?” instead of “What is the biggest risk you see?” They miss critical signals.

5. What a manager decides (customer, safety, cybersecurity, schedule implications) Interview depth

You decide what to learn first. You choose which quick wins matter. You decide which decisions to delay. You decide how to communicate with OEM and internal teams.

Customer implications include trust and responsiveness. OEM expects clarity and stability. Early chaos damages credibility.

Safety implications involve hazard analysis, safety case, and test strategy. You must understand safety before making changes. Early missteps can create gaps.

Cybersecurity implications involve secure diagnostics, debug‑port control, and update flows. You must learn security responsibilities before acting.

Schedule implications involve integration cycles, bottlenecks, and team load. You must diagnose before optimizing. Early changes can slow progress.

Your first 90 days set tone. You show whether you stabilize or disrupt. You show whether you listen or assume.

6. Questions a strong manager asks the team Interview depth

Strong questions reveal system behavior and hidden risks.

  • “What slows us down the most in integration?”
  • “Which steering issues worry you, and why?”
  • “Where do we lack clear decision rights?”
  • “Which tests give us confidence, and which do not?”
  • “Where do safety case assumptions feel weak?”
  • “What is the hardest part of working with OEM?”
  • “Which processes help us, and which hurt us?”
  • “If you could fix one thing tomorrow, what would it be?”

7. Weak answer versus strong answer Interview depth

Weak

“In my first 90 days, I will reorganize teams, rewrite processes, and push for faster delivery. I will introduce new metrics and challenge architecture. I will show quick impact.”

Strong

“In my first 90 days, I will assess architecture, safety case, integration flow, and field data. I will deliver small quick wins that unblock teams. I will avoid major decisions until I understand constraints. I will clarify decision rights and interface ownership. I will build trust with OEM and internal teams before proposing structural changes.”

The weak answer assumes action equals impact. It ignores learning and context. It creates instability.

The strong answer shows discipline. It prioritizes assessment, trust, and targeted action.

8. What the interviewer is testing Working

Interviewers test whether you understand leadership entry. They want to see if you assess before acting. They check whether you know which decisions to avoid early.

They also test your ability to build trust. Do you talk about listening, mapping, and diagnosing? Do you avoid early hero moves?

They test whether you can design a 30‑60‑90 plan. Do you structure learning, diagnosis, and action? Do you ask sharp stakeholder questions?

9. Common mistakes (beginner and executive) Working

Beginners act too fast. They propose fixes before understanding problems. They chase visible wins instead of structural ones.

Beginners also avoid stakeholder conversations. They rely on documents. They miss context and signals.

Executives may assume past experience applies directly. They reuse old playbooks without checking fit. They underestimate local constraints.

Executives may make early commitments to OEM. They promise features or dates without understanding load. They create long‑term pain.

Another mistake is focusing on metrics before understanding behavior. Leaders push dashboards without diagnosing bottlenecks.

11. Exercise with answer notes Expert

Scenario: You join an EPS program with integration delays, unclear decision rights, and rising OEM pressure. Teams expect direction. OEM expects stability. You must design your first 90 days.

Task: Build a 30‑60‑90 plan and list what you ask each stakeholder. Avoid early big decisions.

Answer notes:

  • First 30 days – Assess: Observe integration reviews, calibration meetings, and safety case discussions. Read architecture docs, test plans, and field data. Map decision rights and interface ownership. Identify bottlenecks and risks. Deliver small quick wins: clarify a decision, fix a meeting ritual, unblock a test.
  • Questions for engineers: “What slows you down? Which tests give confidence? Where do interfaces break?”
  • Questions for calibration: “Which steering feel issues matter? Where do software changes surprise you?”
  • Questions for safety: “Which safety case assumptions feel weak? Where do hazards lack evidence?”
  • Questions for diagnostics: “Where does tooling fail? Which logs help or hurt integration?”
  • Questions for OEM: “What worries you most? Which issues hurt trust? What do you expect in the next 90 days?”
  • Next 30 days – Diagnose: Identify structural issues: unclear decision rights, missing tests, weak interface ownership, or overloaded teams. Validate findings with stakeholders. Avoid proposing big changes until diagnosis is complete.
  • Final 30 days – Act: Clarify decision rights. Assign interface owners. Improve integration rituals. Add missing tests. Propose structural changes only with evidence. Communicate clearly with OEM.
  • Decisions to avoid early: Reorganizations, architecture changes, safety case rewrites, major process shifts, or commitments to OEM on dates or features.
  • Outcome: You stabilize the program, build trust, and prepare for deeper changes after 90 days.

This exercise shows whether you can enter a complex EPS program with discipline, clarity, and trust-building. It reveals your ability to assess first, act second, and avoid early mistakes.
